01 · Overview

Overview: the claim and the key numbers

The paper attacks a narrow, load-bearing problem: block-sparse attention saves the attention computation but not the cost of deciding which blocks to keep. The conventional recipe scores every query against all $N/C$ key blocks, so the selection stage costs $O(N^2/C)$ in total — attention itself has become linear while block selection stays quadratic, and at long sequence lengths selection becomes the new bottleneck. PISA replaces the full scan with a pyramid: keys are mean-pooled into $O(\log N)$ levels, and selection walks from the coarsest level down, expanding only the survivors. Routing drops to $O(\log N)$ per query and $O(N\log N)$ over the sequence.

The argument in three steps

Problem. Block-sparse attention runs in two stages: pick $K$ key blocks per query, then attend only inside them. Stage two is linear, but stage one must score all $N/C$ block summaries per query, so it stays $O(N^2/C)$ overall. The longer the sequence, the more block selection looks like full attention again.

Method. Pool keys into a fine-to-coarse pyramid ($C$ per group, then 2 per group), then select in the opposite direction: at each level score only the children of blocks kept at the previous level, keep the top $K$, and expand. Candidates per level are bounded by $gK$ and there are $O(\log N)$ levels, giving $O(\log N)$ routing per query. The block score is a LogSumExp over child summaries rather than an average of child means; Appendix A sandwiches it between the mean score and the parent's true raw-key LSE.

Evidence. At 418M / 1.47B / 2.67B, pretrained on 100B tokens at 4K and continued-pretrained on 10B tokens at 16K, language-modelling loss and commonsense reasoning are comparable to BSA, NSA and HiLS; the six-task containment average is the best among sparse methods at all three scales; the RULER average is clearly above BSA. Selection latency overtakes BSA after 32K and is 9.95× faster at 256K.

The other side of the coin: PISA replaces "scan every key block" with "score a fixed ~$gK$ leaf blocks plus a few intermediate candidates". The first grows with $N$, the second is constant — so on short sequences PISA is actually slower. The paper's own numbers show BSA winning from 4K to 16K, with the crossover between 16K and 32K. This is a mechanism built for long context, not a general speedup.
02 · Background

Background: two stages, and the bottleneck in the first

Self-attention grows quadratically with sequence length, which is the hard wall in long-context modelling. Sparse attention lets each query see only part of the keys; the block-sparse variant groups keys and values into contiguous blocks of $C$ tokens, which keeps the sparse computation inside regular tiled kernels instead of turning it into scattered gathers.

The paper formalises block-sparse attention as two stages. Stage one gives each key block a summary vector $\bar{k}_i=f(K_i)$ — mean pooling or a learned projection — and scores it against the query:

$$s_{t,i}=\frac{q_t^{\top}\bar{k}_i}{\sqrt{d}},\qquad i=1,\dots,M,\quad M=\lceil N/C\rceil \tag{1}$$

Stage two attends over the original keys and values inside the selected blocks:

$$o_t=\frac{\sum_{j\in T_t}\exp\!\left(q_t^{\top}k_j/\sqrt{d}\right)v_j}{\sum_{j\in T_t}\exp\!\left(q_t^{\top}k_j/\sqrt{d}\right)},\qquad T_t=\bigcup_{i\in I_t}\{(i-1)C+1,\dots,\min(iC,N)\} \tag{2}$$

Each query reads at most $KC$ keys, so stage two is $O(N)$. Stage one, however, scores $M=N/C$ blocks per query, accumulating $O(N^2/C)$: the attention cost was cut, the selection cost was not.

Where this sits relative to prior work

The paper positions the literature on two axes — trainable or not, and prefill/decode complexity. The table is worth reading line by line, because it names both PISA's niche and its competitors:

Read down the columns and the claim is sharp: the only row that is simultaneously trainable, $O(N\log N)$ at prefill and $O(\log N)$ at decode is PISA. Log-linear complexity itself is not new — HiP and LLSA both have it — the contribution is making it an end-to-end trainable language-model attention layer with a decode path that keeps the $\log N$ guarantee.

A convention worth noticing: the BSA baseline is the authors' own re-composition of NSA's selected-attention branch with mean-pooled key summaries, not a verbatim reproduction of a published system. NSA keeps its learned-compression and selected-attention branches; HiLS keeps its learnable landmark routing. All methods share the same data, backbone and attention projections (paper §4.1 and §C.1).
03 · Method

Method: a pyramid of keys, selected coarse-to-fine

3.1 Fine to coarse: building the pyramid

Level 0 is the raw keys ($M_0=N$, one token per block); level 1 is the leaf blocks ($M_1=\lceil N/C\rceil$); above that every $g=2$ adjacent blocks merge:

$$M_{\ell+1}=\left\lceil\frac{M_\ell}{g_\ell}\right\rceil,\qquad g_0=C,\qquad g_\ell=g=2\;(\ell\ge 1) \tag{3}$$

Summaries are mean-pooled from their children, starting from $\bar{k}_i^{(0)}=k_i$:

$$\bar{k}_i^{(\ell+1)}=\frac{1}{|\mathrm{Ch}_\ell(i)|}\sum_{r\in\mathrm{Ch}_\ell(i)}\bar{k}_r^{(\ell)},\qquad \ell\ge 0 \tag{4}$$

Because the merge groups are equally sized at every step, the recursion yields the mean of all original keys in the block. Queries stay token-level throughout; they are never pooled.

3.2 Coarse to fine: narrowing the Top-K

Selection runs in the opposite direction. The coarsest level has a single candidate, and each level only processes the children of the previous level's survivors:

$$A_t^{(L)}=\{1\};\qquad s_t^{(\ell)}=\left[s_{t,i}^{(\ell)}\right]_{i\in A_t^{(\ell)}},\quad I_t^{(\ell)}=\mathrm{SelectK}\!\left(A_t^{(\ell)},s_t^{(\ell)}\right),\quad A_t^{(\ell-1)}=\bigcup_{i\in I_t^{(\ell)}}\mathrm{Ch}_{\ell-1}(i) \tag{5}$$
$$\left|I_t^{(\ell)}\right|\le K,\qquad \left|A_t^{(\ell-1)}\right|\le g\left|I_t^{(\ell)}\right|\le gK \tag{6}$$

After the walk, $I_t^{(1)}$ is the set of original key/value blocks used for attention. The pyramid is $L=O(\log N)$ levels tall and each level is capped at $gK$ candidates, so routing costs $O(\log N)$ per query and $O(N\log N)$ over the sequence — the "log-linear" in the title.

One detail carries a lot of weight: if a level has at most $K$ candidates, they are all retained without scoring. The top levels of the pyramid are therefore nearly free, which is what makes the mechanism cheap in practice and not just asymptotically.

04 · Scoring

Scoring: LogSumExp, and why not the mean

Intermediate blocks have no raw keys available, only child summaries. The paper scores them with a LogSumExp over those summaries:

$$s_{t,i}^{(\ell)}=\log\sum_{r\in\mathrm{Ch}_{\ell-1}(i)}\exp\!\left(\frac{q_t^{\top}\bar{k}_r^{(\ell-1)}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\qquad \ell\ge 1 \tag{7}$$

At the leaf level $\bar{k}_r^{(0)}=k_r$, so this degenerates into the exact LSE over original keys. Each candidate expands at most $g$ intermediate children or $C$ raw keys, so scoring stays bounded.

A Taylor expansion explains the choice. For logits $z_1,\dots,z_m$ with mean $\bar z$ and variance $\mathrm{Var}(z)$:

$$s_{\mathrm{PISA}}=\log\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{z_j}=\log m+\bar z+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}(z)+\text{higher-order terms} \tag{8}$$

Dropping $\log m$, truncating at first order gives "mean only" and at second order "mean plus half the variance" — exactly the two ablation variants:

$$s_{\mathrm{PISA-1}}=\bar z,\qquad s_{\mathrm{PISA-2}}=\bar z+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Var}(z) \tag{9}$$

Appendix A then gives a Jensen sandwich. Let block $B$ have $g$ equally sized children $C_1,\dots,C_g$, let $m_j$ be the dot product of the query with child $j$'s summary, and let $F_q(B)$ be the parent's normalised raw-key LSE:

$$\frac{1}{g}\sum_{j=1}^{g}m_j\;\le\;\hat F(B):=\log\!\left(\frac{1}{g}\sum_{j=1}^{g}e^{m_j}\right)\;\le\;F_q(B) \tag{10}$$

The left term is the mean-of-means used by PISA-1, the middle term is PISA's score minus $\log g$, and the right term is the parent's true LSE. In words: taking a LogSumExp over child means sits closer to the true block score than averaging the child means does, because it preserves and amplifies differences between children. It cannot recover variation inside a child; when all logits within a child are equal the bounds meet.

The caveat the paper attaches itself: the comparison is about the numerical error of a block score and, in the paper's words, "does not guarantee more accurate block rankings or recovery of the globally highest-mass leaf blocks" (Appendix A.1). So this is an argument that the score is closer to the truth, not a theorem that selection is better. Selection quality is settled empirically, by the Table 7 diagnostic later on.

4.1 An inconsistency the paper discloses

Following NSA, query heads inside one GQA group share the selected blocks. PISA sums their LSE scores into a single block score:

$$u_{h,t,i}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{h'\in H(h)}s_{h',t,i}^{(\ell)} \tag{11}$$

Appendix A.3 points out that the full-attention reference in the diagnostic instead averages block masses across those heads. Even with exact LSE scores, summing and averaging can rank blocks differently. The paper flags this itself; it is a convention mismatch to keep in mind when reading Table 7, not an error.

05 · Systems

Systems: Triton kernels and the IO ledger

Naively, the level-by-level loop would write and re-read intermediate candidates and give back in memory bandwidth what it saved in FLOPs. The paper's answer is to fuse the intermediate levels into a single kernel, keep candidate indices local to the program, and never materialise a dense query–key score matrix.

5.1 Why training uses two stages and decoding one

Rather than asserting that the kernels are fast, the paper puts the two designs' IO costs side by side. Per KV head, let $G_Q$ query heads share it, $C$ be the block size and $Q_{\text{tile}}$ the query tile.

Two-stage (training/prefill). Each query is tied to at most $gK$ candidate key blocks, so loading its queries costs $O(NgKG_Qd)$; because query tiles run in parallel, each key block is loaded on average $O(CgK/Q_{\text{tile}})$ times, giving a total key IO of $O(NCgKd/Q_{\text{tile}})$. The two-stage leaf-level Q/K IO is therefore

$$O\!\left(NgKd\left(G_Q+\frac{C}{Q_{\text{tile}}}\right)\right) \tag{12}$$

Single-stage walks straight to the leaf level, loading up to $gK$ original key blocks per query, for an extra $O(NgKCd)$ (Eq. 13). The two-stage design wins whenever

$$G_Q+\frac{C}{Q_{\text{tile}}}

With the implementation's $G_Q=16$, $Q_{\text{tile}}=4$ and $C=64$, this is $16+64/4=32<64$ — the condition holds, so training and prefill use the two-stage kernel.

Decoding flips the conclusion. A decoding cluster contains a single query, so there is no cross-query reuse: the two-stage kernel would load $gKC$ keys and reload the query vectors $gK$ times, costing $O(gK(C+G_Q)d)$, while a single-stage kernel reuses the queries already loaded during intermediate selection and needs only $O(gKCd)$. Hence the fused single-stage selector for decoding, which also avoids separate grouping and final-selection launches.

Why this ledger matters: it explains a design choice that is easy to dismiss as an implementation detail — the same algorithm runs on two different kernel structures for training and decoding, for a reason that is quantifiable rather than intuitive. The decision reduces to one inequality (Eq. 14) that can be re-evaluated when $G_Q$, $Q_{\text{tile}}$ or $C$ change.

5.3 Remaining conventions

  • Forced blocks and causal masking: following NSA's default in Flash Linear Attention (Yang & Zhang, 2024), the first, previous and current leaf blocks and their ancestor paths are always retained. During prefill, summaries on the current block's path can contain future keys; those nodes are retained but their scores are not used for ranking, and the attention kernel masks future tokens inside selected blocks separately.
  • Backpropagation: the selected block indices are held fixed in the backward pass — gradients flow through the attention over the selected entries, not through the discrete selection decision.
  • Decode cache: the pyramid stores $O((N/C)d)$ elements per KV head and each level grows by a fixed fraction. Even if every expansion copies the whole buffer, total copying to length $N$ is $O((N/C)d\log N)$, keeping the average decode cost at $O(\log N)$ per generated token.

07 · Long context & efficiency

Long context, selection quality, selection efficiency

7.1 RULER needle-in-a-haystack

Long-context retrieval uses four RULER needle-in-a-haystack families (Hsieh et al., 2024) — single-key, multi-key, multi-query and multi-value — greedily decoded at five lengths from 1K to 16K, on the 2.67B models after 16K continued pretraining.

Flattening the 16K column of Table 3 makes the picture clearer (arithmetic mean over the four families, values from that table, [estimated]): PISA 30.86, PISA-2 32.56 and PISA-1 28.09 all beat BSA's 25.90, but all sit below NSA's 39.99 and Full Attention's 53.50. PISA's retrieval edge over BSA holds at 16K; it is not the strongest method in that column.

7.2 Block-selection diagnostic

This section answers whether the pyramid plus LSE actually selects the right blocks. The design is clean: take a 418M full-attention model (100B tokens pretrained), feed every selector the same query and key tensors, let each pick $K=8$ blocks, and compare against a reference set derived from the full-attention weights. All 24 layers, $C=64$, 100 FDA prompts, query positions $\ge512$ only (where more than eight blocks are eligible). The two metrics are the overlap Recall@K and the retained attention mass ratio.

7.3 Selection latency

Finally, efficiency. What is measured is the latency of the selection stage alone: random BF16 queries and keys, batch 1, 32 query heads, 2 KV heads, $d=64$, $C=64$, $K=8$, $g=2$, dot products accumulated in FP32. Timings include mean-summary construction and all selection stages and exclude attention over the selected blocks.

Efficiency takeaway: this is a win-at-length scorecard. Once selection complexity goes from $O(N^2/C)$ to $O(N\log N)$, the benefit only outweighs PISA's fixed extra work when $N$ is large enough; the measured crossover is between 16K and 32K, and at 256K the gap is nearly 10×. Both questions a reader is likely to ask — "is it faster than BSA?" (much faster at long lengths, slower at short ones) and "by how much?" (9.95× on the selection stage; the end-to-end multiple is not measured, see §8.2) — are answered in this section.
08 · Commentary

Commentary: lineage, limits, and what is not disclosed

8.1 Limitations (the paper's own)

The Limitations section is one short paragraph: computational constraints limited the model sizes and pretraining budgets explored, and both factors can substantially affect performance and the relative gains over baselines; within the settings evaluated, the central finding — pyramid block selection reduces selection complexity while keeping performance competitive — holds. No other limitation is self-reported.

8.3 Not disclosed, worth testing next

  • End-to-end speed and throughput. Figure 3 and Table 4 measure the selection stage and explicitly exclude attention over the selected blocks; the paper reports no step time, tokens/s or end-to-end speedup, and no measured memory footprint. So "9.95× faster" is a selection-stage figure and should not be read as an end-to-end gain. The benchmark hardware is not named either.
  • Modelling quality beyond 64K. 256K appears only in the selection-latency microbenchmark, on random queries and keys. No real model is evaluated at 64K–256K for perplexity or retrieval, yet that is exactly the range where PISA's complexity advantage matters. "Faster at long lengths" has direct evidence; "still good at long lengths" is supported only up to 16K.
  • Pyramid maintenance cost is never isolated. The timings include summary construction, but apart from Figure 3 the paper reports neither the extra memory of the pyramid cache during training and decoding nor a memory comparison against BSA. The pyramid is $O((N/C)d)$ elements per KV head — modest, but unmeasured.
  • Single seed, no error bars. All training and evaluation runs use seed 42. Differences in Tables 2/3/6 are often in the third decimal or a few points (for instance the RULER average: PISA 62.80 against PISA-2 62.92 and NSA 61.07). No repeats and no significance testing, so the stability of the small gaps is unknown.
  • Baseline coverage. Trainable comparisons are BSA, NSA and HiLS only. MoBA (Lu et al., 2025), listed in Table 1, and the SeerAttention, IndexCache, LongCat and InfLLM-V2 references are not evaluated; nor are training-free methods compared for latency at the same lengths.
  • LSE versus its truncated approximations disagree across tables. Full PISA wins the Table 7 diagnostic (90.95 vs PISA-2's 88.24) and the loss columns of Table 2, but on the RULER average PISA-2 (62.92) edges out PISA (62.80). The paper does not discuss this reversal; a claim that exact LSE is better would need RULER explained or repeated.
  • Contribution of the forced blocks to the diagnostic. Recall@8 necessarily includes three forced blocks, which the paper states in the Figure 2 caption, but no net Recall excluding them is given, so a reader cannot tell how much of the "8 slots" is handed over for free.

8.4 Assessment

What the contribution really is. Quadratic block selection is a real engineering bottleneck in long-context systems, and the paper answers it with a structure that is both simple and analysable: build a pooling pyramid and replace "scan every block" with "narrow level by level". The $O(N\log N)$ complexity itself had already been reached by a training-free method (HiP) and by a diffusion-model method (LLSA), so the incremental contribution is threefold: putting hierarchical selection inside a trainable language-model attention layer, replacing the block score with an LSE backed by a sandwich argument, and working out the IO ledger for training/prefill versus decoding and turning it into two Triton kernels. That third point is the most solid — writing out "substitute $G_Q=16$, $Q_{\text{tile}}=4$, $C=64$ and check whether the inequality holds" is far more informative than the customary claim that a kernel is well optimised.

How strong the evidence is. The division of labour is clear: modelling quality rests on three scales and 100B+10B tokens of training, selection quality on a controlled diagnostic over identical tensors, and selection efficiency on a 4K–256K microbenchmark. Each strand stands on its own, but together they do not add up to "PISA is better across the board." Loss is beaten by HiLS; single-key retrieval is beaten by NSA; the RULER average is beaten by PISA's own PISA-2 variant; containment after continued pretraining is beaten by BSA; selection latency below 16K is beaten by BSA. What holds in every experiment is narrower: the six-task containment average during pretraining (best at all three scales) and selection latency above 32K (2.86× from 64K, 9.95× at 256K). The abstract's phrasing — comparable commonsense reasoning, better retrieval — maps onto exactly those two, and does not overreach. That is an honest abstract.

Where it applies. The value of the mechanism grows with sequence length; below 16K it is outright slower. Its target is long-context prefill and long-sequence decoding. A reader deploying at 4K–16K gets no reason to adopt it from this paper; at 64K and beyond, the fact that selection grows quadratically with length is genuinely removed, and the quality cost at 16K looks acceptable in these experiments.

What is still open. The most important question is: if selection is 10× faster, how much faster is the end-to-end forward pass? That depends on the share of time taken by attention over the selected blocks, and the paper does not measure it, so how much of the benefit is realised remains open. Next is whether the pyramid's memory and copying overhead is really amortised as $O(\log N)$ inside a real training loop, and whether quality holds beyond 64K — both need measurements outside this paper's configuration.

PISAPyramid Sparse AttentionThis paper's method: a pyramid of key levels plus coarse-to-fine level-by-level Top-K selection.
