CoWindow Attention: turning "full causal coverage" from a per-head duplicate into a collective property of the head ensemble

Every KV head shares one near window and one prefix-sink window; the remaining long-range causal distances are cut into complementary intervals, one per KV head. Individually each head is sparse at distance; collectively they are dense over the whole causal prefix — with no learned router or indexer, one position-defined window rule shared by the training forward/backward passes, inference prefill and autoregressive decoding, and window assignment by global KV-head index so that it aligns with tensor-parallel KV-head sharding.

01 · Overview

Overview

This paper does exactly one thing: it replaces the implicit convention that every attention head must repeatedly see the complete causal history with the convention that a group of KV heads collectively sees the complete causal history. Each head becomes sparse at distance; the duplicated long-range query-key connections are removed. The authors' answer is that as long as this "collective coverage" is complete, retrieval and language-modeling quality barely move.

The construction is light. All KV heads share a near window and a prefix-sink window; the remaining long-range distances are split evenly into $H_k$ complementary intervals, one per KV head. Any key position is therefore always directly visible to some head in the layer, but never has to be visible to all of them. The paper calls this property collective coverage. Three things to remember:
① It does not remove the quadratic term. For fixed $H_k$, full-sequence attention stays $O(N^2)$; what is saved is the cross-head duplication of long-range connections. That is exactly why the FLOPs reduction is only 3.1% in pre-training but 28.5% in the long-context stage: the longer the sequence, the smaller the shared window's share.
② The selling point is the absence of selection cost. Visible blocks are computed from position (and the global KV-head index) rather than retrieved: no block pooling or TopK as in MoBA, no lightning indexer as in DSA. The auxiliary state is $O(H_k)$, independent of sequence length. On long sequences this is what pulls ahead of the baselines.
③ It has a hard prerequisite: $H_k$ must be reasonably large. The number of complementary intervals is the number of KV heads, and the paper states that at $H_k=1$ full coverage degenerates to dense causal attention. That puts the design in tension with the MLA/MQA direction of trading KV heads for cache size.
02 · Background

Background: what gets optimized, and what does not

2.1 FlashAttention optimizes IO, not duplication

Long-context requirements — long-document understanding, multi-turn reasoning, repository-level code generation — push sequences from thousands to hundreds of thousands of tokens, and attention dominates the cost. Work such as FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) improves IO efficiency through tiling, fusion and online softmax, but it does not change the attention pattern: every head still runs QK and PV over the entire causal prefix. The same distant key is therefore read and multiplied once per head — in the KV dimension $H_k$ times, and in the QO dimension up to $H_q$ times. That is pure structural redundancy, and no amount of kernel engineering removes it.

2.2 Three existing routes to sparsity, and what each costs

The paper groups prior work into three families and names the price of each; this sets up the operator benchmark later on:

  • Fixed or local windows (Longformer, Beltagy et al., 2020; SWA): regular and fast, but every head gets the same finite horizon and direct access to more distant tokens is simply lost;
  • Structured blocks and global tokens (Child et al., 2019; Longformer): only predetermined long-range paths are kept;
  • Dynamic selection (Seer, NSA, MoBA, DSA, InfLLMv2 and others): more flexible, but it introduces a separate selection problem and usually breaks the regular window structure that makes sparse attention cheap — so the router or indexer brings its own FLOPs and auxiliary state.

The paper then makes a point that is easy to miss: two methods can be given the same selected-token budget and still differ substantially end to end, because the selection itself costs money. Table 3's asymptotic ledger and Figure 3's measured curves both cash this out.

2.3 Why distributing across heads is plausible

The starting observation is head heterogeneity: heads spontaneously develop distinct roles, with both local and long-range behaviours (Barbero et al., 2025, and the line of work cited there). If heads were never homogeneous to begin with, then "every head must attend to the entire history" is not self-evidently necessary — it suffices that every historical position remains visible to at least one head for each query.

The closest upstream work is SPAttention (ref. [66]), which distributes causal-distance regions across MHA heads while preserving aggregate causal connectivity. The paper's framing of the relationship is careful and worth quoting in substance: it generalizes this into a design principle that can be evaluated systematically — an attention layer keeps access to the full causal history collectively, without requiring every head to attend to every position — and instantiates it under GQA, where the complementary windows live on KV heads rather than on all heads. A footnote draws the boundary explicitly: SPAttention's published exclusive bands differ from its released shared-local implementation MEHC, which does not provide the long-context GQA, decoding, or tensor-parallel execution studied here.

Put together, the paper's question is: can distributing access to the causal history across KV heads preserve model quality and long-range retrieval while actually reducing training and inference cost? Note that "actually executable" is part of the question — which is why a good share of the paper is about tensor parallelism, block traversal and decoding rather than about the attention pattern alone.

03 · Method

Method: how the windows are cut, why coverage holds, what it costs

3.1 One distance definition spanning training and inference

First the engineering trap is closed. Training uses a full sequence with a causal mask; prefill may have $N_q \neq N_k$; decoding has $N_q = 1$ against a cache of length $N_k$. The paper defines a bottom-right-aligned causal distance that unifies all three:

$$\delta(i_q, i_k) := i_q + (N_k - N_q) - i_k \tag{1}$$

When $N_q = N_k$ this reduces to the familiar $i_q - i_k$, and a key is causally available to a query exactly when $\delta(i_q, i_k) \ge 0$. Every window below (near, sink, long-range) is then expressed purely in terms of $\delta$, so training, prefill and decoding need no separate rules — a structural advantage over dynamic sparse methods, which typically need special handling at decode steps (a newly generated token is not yet in the index, and so on).

The second interface is GQA ($H_q$ an integer multiple of $H_k$): QO head $h$ uses the K/V tensors and window assignment of KV head $h_k = \lfloor h H_k / H_q \rfloor$. Those $H_q/H_k$ QO heads form a group that shares keys, values and the visible-key set while computing weights from distinct queries. The group concept recurs throughout, because a shared visible set means how often a distant key is duplicated depends on the group size, not on $H_q$.

3.2 Three windows: shared sink, shared near, exclusive long-range

First fix how much history remains outside the shared windows, and where the cut points go:

$$L := \max(N_k - w_{\text{sink}} - w_{\text{near}},\, 0), \qquad b_{h_k} := \left\lfloor \frac{h_k\, L}{H_k} \right\rfloor, \quad h_k = 0,\dots,H_k \tag{2}$$

Writing $\delta = \delta(i_q,i_k)$, the three windows are:

$$\begin{aligned} W^{\text{near}}_{h_k}(i_q) &:= \{\, i_k : 0 \le \delta < w_{\text{near}} \,\} &&\text{(near, shared by all heads)}\\ W^{\text{sink}}_{h_k}(i_q) &:= \{\, i_k : 0 \le i_k < w_{\text{sink}},\ \delta \ge 0 \,\} &&\text{(prefix sink, shared by all heads)}\\ W^{\text{long}}_{h_k}(i_q) &:= \{\, i_k : w_{\text{near}} + b_{h_k} \le \delta < w_{\text{near}} + b_{h_k+1} \,\} &&\text{(long-range, exclusive to one head)} \end{aligned}$$

The visible-key set is their union:

$$A_{h_k}(i_q) := W^{\text{near}}_{h_k}(i_q) \cup W^{\text{sink}}_{h_k}(i_q) \cup W^{\text{long}}_{h_k}(i_q) \tag{3}$$

So each head sees the shared recent context plus the shared start of the sequence plus one long-range interval of its own. The width of $W^{\text{long}}$ is $b_{h_k+1} - b_{h_k}$; equal widths (adjacent cut points $L/H_k$ apart) are the default, chosen for load balance during decoding. In the algorithm interface each head's windows collapse into a four-tuple $w_{h_k} = (w_{\text{sink}},\, w_{\text{near}},\, b_{h_k},\, b_{h_k+1}-b_{h_k})$ — sink width, near width, the gap after the near window, and the long-range width. The same interface admits head-specific widths and gaps, but this work only uses equal widths.

3.3 Collective coverage: the union really does tile the causal prefix

Taking the union over the $H_k$ heads:

$$\bigcup_{h_k=0}^{H_k-1} A_{h_k}(i_q) \;=\; \{\, i_k \in \{0,\dots,N_k-1\} : \delta(i_q, i_k) \ge 0 \,\} \tag{4}$$

The right-hand side is the complete causal prefix — every key position lies inside at least one head's direct view. But the paper immediately adds a caveat that deserves to be quoted verbatim in spirit: full causal coverage guarantees direct access to token positions, but it does not imply the same head-specific interactions or outputs as FullAttn. The reason is that each QO head applies softmax over its own visible-key set — the normalizer changes. CoWA is therefore not an approximation-equivalent of FullAttn; it is a different attention distribution that happens to leave the "accessible information" dimension intact. The coverage ablation in §5 exists to quantify exactly this difference.

3.4 The cost ledger: the near window is what gets duplicated

Consider the final query position $i_q = N_q-1$ in the common regime $w_{\text{sink}}+w_{\text{near}} \le N_k$. Sink, near and long-range are disjoint there, so the total number of visible keys summed across KV heads is:

$$\sum_{h_k=0}^{H_k-1} |A_{h_k}(N_q-1)| \;=\; H_k\,(w_{\text{sink}} + w_{\text{near}}) \;+\; L \tag{5}$$

The reading is direct: the shared windows are repeated by every KV head and contribute $H_k(w_{\text{sink}}+w_{\text{near}})$, whereas each long-range key belongs to exactly one KV-head support set and the whole long-range span contributes only $L$. Dense causal attention's counterpart is $H_k N_k$. Dividing by $H_k$ gives the average number of visible keys per KV head; with equal query groups, multiplying either summed count by $H_q/H_k$ converts it into actual QO-head query-key connections.

The paper also records several boundary cases, all worth keeping:

  • the count above is for the final query; full-sequence training accumulates support sizes over all positions, and earlier queries are clipped by causality and may have overlapping windows, so the count drops — actual work can differ across heads even when widths are equal;
  • if $w_{\text{sink}}+w_{\text{near}} > N_k$ the shared windows already cover the causal prefix, the per-head count is capped at $N_k$, and the long-range windows do nothing;
  • for fixed $H_k$, full-sequence attention remains quadratic; the saving comes from fewer duplicated query-key connections;
  • cross-head decomposition requires $H_k > 1$; at $H_k=1$ full coverage reduces to dense causal attention — this is the design's hard boundary.

Now the tensor-parallel side. That alignment is not required, but it shows why global-index assignment is the necessary choice: assigning by local index would give every rank "long-range band 0", collapsing the complementarity between ranks and breaking the design under TP.

The long-range redundancy equals that group size, whereas the near window's redundancy is $H_q$ — this is precisely where "fewer duplicates" is cashed in.

04 · Execution

Execution: one rule covering forward, backward, decoding and tensor parallelism

The second half of the method section introduces almost no new concepts. It is there to show that a single position-defined window rule can run every path of training and inference without special cases — which is exactly where dynamic sparse methods are most expensive, since they typically need a separate approximation or index maintenance step for decoding, for the backward pass and for distribution.

4.1 Forward: visit only the legal blocks

The forward operator serves both the training forward pass and prefill. Q/O and K/V are stored head-major with shapes $Q,O \in \mathbb{R}^{H_q \times N_q \times d_h}$ and $K,V \in \mathbb{R}^{H_k \times N_k \times d_h}$ (for notational simplicity the paper assumes $Q$ is pre-scaled by $1/\sqrt{d_h}$). The token dimension is split into QO blocks $I_i$ and KV blocks $J_j$, and each QO block only touches the KV-block set $\mathcal{J}_{h_k}(i)$ that contains at least one legal pair under Equation 3:

4.2 Backward: traverse the inverse relation, exactly

The backward operator flips the traversal: for each KV block $J_j$ it computes the set of QO blocks $\mathcal{I}_{h_k}(j)$ that have legal pairs with it — the inverse of the forward visibility relation. The visible-key set is the same as in the forward pass, so this is not an approximate gradient.

4.3 Decoding: the cut points slide every step

Autoregressive decoding is the $N_q = 1$ case of the forward pass: $\delta(0, i_k) = N_k - 1 - i_k$, and $N_k$ equals the number of cached tokens plus one for the current token, so the near window naturally lands on the most recent KV-cache entries. The critical detail is that the cut points are recomputed from the current key length at every step — $b_{h_k}$ in Equation 2 depends on $N_k$, so equal-width long-range bands slide smoothly as the sequence grows rather than requiring re-indexing as fixed block partitions would. Visible KV blocks can be distributed across split-KV programs and merged through standard partial online-softmax states; how the splits are scheduled sets parallelism and load balance but does not change the window rule itself.

4.4 Tensor parallelism: global indexing is a necessary design choice

This subsection is the paper's clearest "it has to be done this way" argument. Let $TP$ be the tensor-parallel degree (with $TP \le H_k$, and the $H_k$ KV heads sharded evenly and contiguously). For local head $h_{\text{local}}$ on rank $r$:

$$h_{\text{global}} = r\,H_k^{\text{local}} + h_{\text{local}}, \qquad H_k^{\text{local}} = H_k / TP \tag{6}$$

The breakpoints in Equation 2 are always evaluated with the global index $h_{\text{global}}$ and the global $H_k$, in training and inference alike. Each rank therefore receives complementary long-range intervals rather than every rank starting from "band 0". The backward pass works the same way: each rank traverses the inverse relation for its local KV heads only, and the global indices keep the sparse pattern aligned across ranks.

Three boundaries are stated here, and they map directly onto the measurement conventions later:

  • Persistent KV cache and operator peak memory are different things. In the model-level evaluations each rank still keeps the full history of its local KV heads resident in HBM; window-aware offloading and prefetching to reduce HBM-resident KV storage are explicitly future work. The peaks measured in §6 are along the operator path, not serving-time cache occupancy.
  • $TP > H_k$ replicates logical KV heads, in which case each rank stores more than $1/TP$ of the global KV cache.
  • KV-head sharding partitions the cache across ranks, whereas visiting only visible KV blocks reduces the decoding operator's working memory — the two should not be conflated.
The transferable lesson here is "fold the distributed constraint into the formula". Defining breakpoints by global index looks like adding a subscript, but it is what makes complementarity an invariant across ranks; otherwise the same logical head would receive different windows under different TP configurations, and the attention pattern used in training would no longer match the one used in deployment. In dynamic sparse methods that mismatch typically shows up as "training runs, deployment loses points".
05 · Experiments I

Experiment I: isolating coverage as a single variable

5.1 Ablation design: same window widths, different numbers of distinct long-range windows

To show that CoWA's benefit comes from complementary allocation rather than from wider windows, the cleanest possible experiment freezes the per-head window width and varies only how many distinct long-range windows are spread across the eight KV heads:

  • 1 distinct window × 8 heads (duplicated) → 12.5% long-range coverage
  • 2 windows × 4 heads → 25%
  • 4 windows × 2 heads → 50%
  • 8 windows × 1 head (CoWA) → 100%

One implementation detail is easy to overlook but matters: the duplicated layouts repeat the windows closest to the near window ($W_1,\dots,W_m$). The control arms therefore receive the most recent long-range segments — the freshest information — while CoWA must also claim the most distant ones. The bias runs against CoWA, and it still wins, which makes the conclusion stronger and is worth mentioning whenever the table is cited.

The paper's conclusion is blunt: 8K recall rises monotonically as duplicated windows are replaced by complementary ones (21.32 → 32.92 → 52.32 → 89.73), full coverage nearly matches FullAttn (89.97), and the near-only SWA reference collapses to 5.34%. These numbers support the value of distributing complementary long-range access across KV heads.

5.2 Controlled associative recall: stronger opponents

The second experiment switches to the standard associative recall task (the Zoology setup of Arora et al., 2024): 256 key-value pairs, randomized bindings with no semantic shortcut, 250K training and 1K test examples, 100 epochs, sequence lengths 1,024 → 8,192 and $d_{model}$ from 64 to 512. The opponents span dense, local, structured and dynamic sparsity: FullAttn, SWA, Seer, InfLLMv2, NSA, MoBA and DSA.

The budget-matching convention is worth stating explicitly: all four sequence lengths are run at per-query budgets of 1,024 / 1,152 / 1,408 / 1,920 [organized from Appendix A.1]. The check in §3.5 shows these are exactly $1024 + (N_k-1024)/8$ — i.e. every opponent competes at the same budget CoWA needs for full coverage, so CoWA does not win by spending more. CoWA itself uses $w_{\text{sink}}=1$ and $w_{\text{near}}=1023$, a shared width of 1,024 tokens.

One scope boundary must be stated as well: the paper explicitly does not insert random padding or additional noise tokens. The task is therefore retrieval in the pure sense, without the extra job of filtering distractors — a point the commentary returns to.

How to read these two results: Table 1 answers "how much is coverage worth?", Figure 2 answers "how does it compare with stronger opponents?". Table 1 is the more persuasive of the two because it reduces the variables to coverage alone. Figure 2 reports, at 8K and $d_{model}=512$, CoWA 89.73% against FullAttn 89.97%, with DSA, MoBA and NSA at 53.71%, 50.12% and 25.23% and the remainder near 10% [organized from the main text].

6.3 The curves: at long lengths CoWA pulls ahead by not doing selection

Concretely at 128K [organized from Table 6]: CoWA forward 21.758 ms, backward 51.509 ms, decode 0.015 ms against FullAttn's 159.987 / 441.230 / 0.045 ms — operator-level speedups of 7.4× / 8.6× / 3.0×. On memory, CoWA's forward and backward peaks (580 / 1,804 MiB) are digit-for-digit identical to FullAttn's, while its decode peak of 8.441 MiB is 7.6× below FullAttn's 64.004 MiB. MoBA's backward pass exceeds the per-rank memory limit at 128K; DSA's 128K forward takes 93.671 ms despite a selected budget a quarter of CoWA's, which the paper attributes to indexer cost.

Measurement caveat (important): these are operator-level end-to-end timings, not full model training-step times. The paper spells out what each path includes: MoBA covers block-representative construction, router logits, TopK, index construction, data rearrangement and output merging; DSA covers BF16 indexer inputs, FP8 quantization, indexer logits, TopK/indices and sparse MLA; CoWA covers its fused window-range and masking logic. Model-level QKV/output projections, MLP layers, communication and optimizer updates are out of scope. The 7.4×/8.6×/3.0× should therefore be read as attention-operator speedups, not machine-level ones; the model-level payoff is the training FLOPs of §7.
07 · Experiments III

Experiment III: scaling from 0.6B to 14B, and 14B/32B model evaluation

7.1 Configuration: five sizes, two training stages

Training runs on 128 H100 GPUs with Megatron-LM; the data is SmolLM-Corpus, the tokenizer is Qwen3's, the optimizer is AdamW with a WSD schedule, and the random seed is fixed at 42. Each size is trained in two stages: pre-training (PT, sequence length 4,096) and long-context training (LCT, 32,768), where LCT initializes from the final PT checkpoint at a constant learning rate. Across the five sizes — 0.6B / 1.7B / 4B / 8B / 14B — layers go from 28 to 40, $d_{model}$ from 1024 to 5120 and QO heads from 16 to 40, while the KV-head count stays fixed at 8 throughout. That is worth noting now; the commentary returns to it.

7.2 The FLOPs ledger: why pre-training saves 3.1% and long context saves 28.5%

The x axis is cumulative training FLOPs as reported by Megatron-LM, covering the complete model computation. To expose the attention-specific contribution, the paper additionally measures the legal attention connections actually executed under each configuration instead of assigning the final-query budget to every query. With $\bar{s}$ the mean number of selected keys per query and per QO head, the sequence-dependent core-attention cost per token and layer is

$$C_{\text{core}} = 8\,\bar{s}\,h\,d_{qk} + 6\,\bar{s}\,h\,d_{v}$$

where the QK side carries a factor of 8 because the backward pass recomputes scores and the PV side 6 (one multiply-accumulate counts as 2 FLOPs). FullAttn has $\bar{s} = (n+1)/2$ over the causal triangle; CoWA's measured values are $\bar{s} = 1{,}040.17$ (4K pre-training) and $\bar{s} = 2{,}930.08$ (32K long-context training). MoBA and DSA use their causally clipped selected supports, with routing and indexing costs counted in their totals.

Hence the paper's central efficiency claim: at 14B, CoWA's pre-training perplexity matches FullAttn's and the two differ by less than 0.01 after long-context training, while total training FLOPs fall by 3.1% (4K stage) and 28.5% (32K stage). The source of the asymmetry is clear: dense attention's cost grows with sequence length, whereas in the 32K stage CoWA achieves full coverage on a per-QO-head budget of 4,992 with no router or indexer FLOPs at all. In pre-training the sequence is only 4K, the shared window of 1024 occupies a quarter of it, and only 3,072 tokens' worth of long-range cost is on the table — so the saving is naturally limited.

7.3 Model evaluation: four 14B variants, and 32B through continued training

Evaluation comes in two groups: at 14B, all four attention variants from the scaling-law study; at 32B, only FullAttn and CoWA, both produced by a separate long-context continued-training run (64B tokens, sequence 32,768, batch 4M, constant learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, 64 layers, $d_{model}$ 5,120, 64 QO heads, 8 KV heads). Every task is run with five seeds (0, 42, 233, 666, 1234) and reported as mean ± standard deviation, using the EleutherAI LM Evaluation Harness on 8 H100 GPUs.

Section 7 in three sentences: ① across 0.6B→14B, CoWA buys perplexity indistinguishable from FullAttn for less training compute; ② the resulting models are "comparable" rather than "better" on knowledge, reasoning and long-context retrieval, with per-task wins and losses; ③ almost all of the FLOPs saving comes from the long-context stage (28.5% vs 3.1%), which matches the motivation exactly — what CoWA removes is the long-range connection cost that dense attention recomputes at long sequences.
08 · Commentary

Commentary: three strengths, five boundaries, and a list of things to test next

8.1 Three design decisions worth learning from

First, coverage was turned into a single ablatable variable. Most sparse-attention papers ablate by "removing component A and measuring the drop", which is not a clean variable. CoWA freezes the per-head window width and varies only how many distinct long-range windows are spread across the eight KV heads, so four coverage levels — 12.5% / 25% / 50% / 100% — map directly onto accuracy (Table 1). Better still, the duplicated layouts repeat the segments closest to the current query, giving the control arms the freshest information. That bias runs against CoWA, and it still wins.

Second, "selection overhead" was turned from a slogan into a ledger. The paper keeps insisting that two methods with the same selected-token count can differ substantially end to end. Table 3 lays out MoBA's $O(n^2hd_{qk}/B)$ and DSA's $O(n^2h_Id_I)$ asymptotically, adds an "auxiliary state" column, and Figure 3 then cashes it out in measurements. This chain gives the long-sequence reversal a mechanism rather than leaving it as a nicer-looking curve.

Third, the distributed constraint was folded into the formula. Breakpoints are evaluated with the global KV-head index (Equation 6) rather than the local one. The cost is a subscript; the benefit is that complementarity becomes an invariant across ranks — and when $TP = H_k = 8$ it yields the tidy division of one long-range band per rank [estimated; see §3.5]. Switching to local indices would give every rank "band 0" and break the design under TP — an easy trap for an implementer to fall into.

8.3 Boundaries worth keeping in mind

① $H_k$ is a hard constraint, and it points against the current architectural current. The number of complementary intervals is the number of KV heads. The paper fixes $H_k = 8$ throughout, while a number of efficient architectures are moving toward fewer KV heads or a single latent KV — the paper's own DSA baseline uses one latent head. At $H_k=1$ collective coverage degenerates to dense causal attention, as the paper itself states. So how much is left at $H_k = 2$ or $4$? Cutting distant history into four bands instead of eight means doubling the long-range window per head to keep full coverage, which also raises the shared windows' duplicated share. No $H_k$ sensitivity curve is given, and that parameter is what decides whether the design generalizes.

② Coverage is not equivalence, and how the gap behaves at longer sequences is untested. The paper supplies the caveat itself: full causal coverage guarantees direct access, but not the same head-specific interactions or outputs as FullAttn. Mechanically it is clear why — each QO head normalizes over its own visible set, so the softmax normalizer changes and CoWA is a different distribution. At 8K the measured gap is 0.24 points (89.73 vs 89.97). But quality is only evaluated to 32K native (plus YaRN 128K), and associative recall stops at 8K, whereas the efficiency advantage is largest at 128K. In other words, whether the distributional difference from incomplete-per-head coverage grows with length is not covered by the evaluation range, and the uncovered range is exactly where the design is fastest.

③ Long-range redundancy is exactly 1. By Equation 5 each long-range key falls inside one KV head's support set, so how many QO heads directly see a distant token equals the group size $H_q/H_k$ (2 at 0.6B, 5 at 14B, 8 at 32B), while a near-window token is visible to all $H_q$ heads. That is the mechanism that saves duplicates, but it also makes distant information a single-point read: no multi-head redundancy, no voting, no "several heads corroborating the same distant evidence". The retrieval task explicitly adds no random padding or noise, so whether a single-point read suffices under distractors is untested. This is the experiment set I would most like to see added.

④ Training-time load imbalance is not addressed. Equal-width allocation is stated to serve decoding load balance; equal-area (or equal-work) allocation is "outside the scope of this work". The paper also acknowledges that even with equal widths, heads' actual work can differ (earlier queries have their support sets clipped by causality). In full-sequence training this means attention work can differ across ranks within one TP group, while ranks are synchronized — that kind of imbalance eats directly into the efficiency gain. What is reported is operator-level synchronized wall-clock, with no per-head load or inter-rank wait distribution.

⑤ The efficiency numbers need their scope read carefully. The 7.4×/8.6×/3.0× and the memory comparisons are all attention-operator-level: QKV/output projections, MLP, communication and optimizer updates are excluded, as the paper lists explicitly. The four methods also use different KV-head configurations (CoWA 8, MoBA 64, DSA a single latent head), so the memory curves are not an equal-head-count comparison — the paper discloses rather than dodges this, but a reader should still treat them as "each method in its native configuration", not as a pure mechanism comparison. The 8.4 MiB decoding figure is an operator peak, not resident KV cache at serving time; §2.3 of the paper goes out of its way to distinguish the two, and window-aware offload/prefetch is listed as future work.

8.4 Not disclosed, worth testing next

  • The window split for the scaling-law and long-context stages is not disclosed. The paper gives the per-QO-head total budgets (1,408 / 4,992) and, for associative recall, $w_{\text{sink}}=1$, $w_{\text{near}}=1023$; how the long-context and operator-benchmark configurations divide sink / near / long-range is not itemized. The check in §3.5 shows every disclosed budget lands exactly on "$1024$ shared + eight equal bands = full coverage" [estimated], but the paper never states that split outright, so a reproducer cannot verify the window parameters.
  • The gap between the measured $\bar{s}$ and the nominal budget is left unexplained. The paper reports $\bar{s} = 1{,}040.17$ (4K PT) and $2{,}930.08$ (32K LCT) against nominal per-QO-head budgets of 1,408 and 4,992. In principle the difference comes from causal clipping, window overlap and sequence packing, but no breakdown is given, so a reader cannot reconcile the 3.1% / 28.5% FLOPs savings item by item. A "budget → actual support size → FLOPs" reconciliation table would close this.
  • No quality curves at longer native sequence lengths. Quality is measured at 32K native / YaRN 128K while the efficiency advantage is largest at 128K; native 64K–128K long-context training with RULER evaluation would be the direct test of "coverage does not degrade at extreme lengths".
  • No $H_k$ sensitivity. Everything is fixed at eight KV heads. A coverage–quality–efficiency curve for $H_k \in \{2,4,8,16\}$ is the single most informative experiment for scoping the design.
  • 32B is compared only against FullAttn and is outside the scaling law. The 32B answer comes from a separate continued-training experiment with just two rows; no 32B results for MoBA/DSA, and NSA appears only in the 8K associative recall. That leaves "does CoWA's advantage over dynamic sparsity survive scale?" without evidence.
  • Retrieval under distractors, and training-stability diagnostics. Associative recall explicitly omits random padding and noise; and the effect of heterogeneous per-head visible sets on gradient scale and per-head load is visible only through final perplexity and one standard-deviation ring, with no intermediate diagnostics.
One-line placement: CoWA reads less like "making attention sparse" and more like re-ordering the cost structure of sparse attention. Using coverage as an ablatable dimension, it trades duplicated long-range connections for complementary division of labour, paying with single-point reads and a dependence on $H_k$. Within the range it measured, the trade is favourable — and the implementation rule is simple enough to land on training, decoding and tensor parallelism at once.
